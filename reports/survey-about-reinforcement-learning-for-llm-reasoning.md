# Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning: A Survey

## TL;DR
- Reinforcement learning (RL) significantly enhances LLM reasoning capabilities, with minimal RL training (e.g., single-example RLVR) yielding substantial improvements on mathematical benchmarks [1].
- Algorithm stabilizations like Advantage Clipped Policy Optimization (ACPO) and EasyPPO address critic instability and improve training efficiency in LLM RL [2][3].
- Integrating RL with tree search (e.g., MCTS-RAG) and inference-time strategies like self-consistency boosts reasoning performance without requiring massive scale [4][5].
- Verifiable reward settings (RLVR) and novel benchmarks (ConsensusBench, CircuitLens) reveal challenges such as reward hacking, strategy collapse, and verifier error dependence that limit RL effectiveness [6][7][8][9][10].

## Background
Large language models (LLMs) exhibit emergent reasoning abilities but remain limited on complex multi-step tasks. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) pioneered the alignment of LLMs with human preferences, demonstrating that 1.3B InstructGPT models could outperform 175B GPT-3 despite far fewer parameters [11]. This established RL as a powerful post-training paradigm. Subsequent work adapted RL to reasoning domains by using verifiable rewards (e.g., correctness of math answers) instead of human labels, leading to Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) and Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback (RLAIF) frameworks. Early results showed that RL fine-tuning could significantly improve mathematical reasoning, setting the stage for extensive research on algorithmic innovations, integration with search methods, and rigorous evaluation on reasoning benchmarks.

## Theme 1: Foundational RL Methods for Reasoning
The application of RL to LLM reasoning builds directly on RLHF foundations but shifts to verifiable reward signals. InstructGPT demonstrated that RLHF could dramatically improve model alignment and reasoning with minimal parameter increases [11]. RLVR extends this paradigm by using automatically verifiable outcomes (e.g., unit test correctness) as rewards, enabling scalable self-improvement. Remarkably, research shows that a single carefully selected training example can elevate a Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B model's performance on MATH500 from 36.0% to 73.6%, matching results from much larger datasets [1]. This efficiency stems from the policy gradient loss and entropy promotion, which encourages exploration and self-reflection. However, RLAIF introduces risks of reward hacking, where the policy exploits systematic errors in an AI judge; adversarial debate training mitigates this by pitting a generator against a critic judged by a weaker LLM, reducing reward hacking in mathematical tasks [6]. These foundational works establish that RL, even with minimal data, can unlock reasoning capabilities, but also highlight sensitivity to reward signal quality.

## Theme 2: Algorithmic Innovations and Stabilization Techniques
Standard PPO faces instability in LLM RL due to critic failure modes, prompting algorithmic refinements. EasyPPO identifies that filtering truncated rollouts shifts the objective to reward conditioned on completion, destabilizing training [2]. Advantage Clipped Policy Optimization (ACPO) further stabilizes training by clipping the product of importance sampling ratios and advantages, directly addressing the core variance source in policy gradients [3]. Beyond PPO, constraint-aware methods like CM-DPO replace binary preference signals with continuous margins from symbolic verifiers, scaling margins by violation severity to separate hard and soft constraints [12]. Federated adaptations (Fed-GRPO) enable privacy-preserving reasoning training by leveraging reward statistics without sharing raw data [13]. These innovations collectively improve training stability, efficiency, and applicability of RL for reasoning, moving beyond vanilla PPO toward more robust and specialized optimizers.

## Theme 3: Integration with Search and Inference-Time Strategies
RL synergizes with explicit search and inference-time computation to enhance reasoning. Tree search methods, particularly Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), improve reasoning by structuring inference as search over partial reasoning states, with works like MCTS-RAG demonstrating performance comparable to larger models through structured thinking [14][5]. Self-consistency decoding, which samples multiple reasoning paths and selects the most consistent answer, provides significant improvements with minimal overhead and complements RL by exploring diverse solution paths [4]. Prompt-based RL approaches, such as evolving functional repertoires, avoid costly parameter updates while achieving competitive performance on knowledge-intensive tasks, raising questions about whether prompt optimization can replace RL for continual learning [15]. However, studies on in-context reinforcement learning (ICRL) find that while models can read trajectory-reward pairs, rewards have no learning effect through controlled experiments, challenging the notion that in-context learning can serve as a substitute for explicit RL [16]. These findings suggest that RL's greatest impact may come from combining explicit policy optimization with search and sampling strategies at inference time.

## Theme 4: Evaluation, Benchmarks, and Emerging Challenges
Progress in RL for LLM reasoning is measured through specialized benchmarks and reveals persistent challenges. Process-verified RL for theorem proving using the Lean proof assistant supplies dense, tactic-level feedback, demonstrating RL's applicability to formal reasoning domains [17]. Data selection signals like CircuitLens, derived from reasoning-sensitive attention heads, improve RLVR efficiency by identifying high-potential training examples without requiring rollouts [8]. ConsensusBench addresses sparse final-answer rewards in GRPO by densifying outcome rewards through intermediate-step feedback, aiming to capture step-wise contributions in longer reasoning trajectories [9]. Analysis of verifier errors within GRPO groups shows significant dependence (pooled correlation of 0.530), reducing effective sample size and highlighting the importance of error modeling [10]. Despite advances, challenges remain: GRPO-style algorithms can exhibit late-stage strategy collapse, where effective reasoning capacity contracts harmfully rather than through benign pruning [7]; entropy collapse in GRPO weakens exploration, mitigated by selectively using rollouts for policy updates [18]; and reward hacking persists in RLAIF settings without adversarial mitigation [6]. These evaluation insights underscore that robust RL for reasoning requires not only algorithmic innovation but also careful reward design, benchmarking, and awareness of failure modes.

## Trends and open problems
Recent trends show a shift toward minimal, high-impact RL interventions (e.g., single-example RLVR) and hybrid methods that combine RL with supervised fine-tuning and inference-time strategies [1][15]. There is growing interest in verifiable reward settings for domains beyond mathematics, such as theorem proving and visual reasoning, supported by benchmarks like Lean-RL and RISEBench [17][19]. However, open problems include developing RL algorithms resistant to strategy collapse and reward hacking, better characterizing the relationship between RL and in-context learning, and creating more data-efficient verifier training methods. Additionally, scaling RL to broader reasoning domains while maintaining stability remains a key challenge, as does understanding the long-term effects of RL on model generalization and catastrophic forgetting. Future work will likely focus on theoretical foundations of RL for LLMs, integration with symbolic verifiers and search, and benchmarks that faithfully measure multi-step reasoning improvement.
